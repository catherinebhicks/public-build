If the coach is going to decide whether an answer is missing something
important, it needs to know what "important" means for the person answering.
A strong junior answer and a strong senior answer to the same question
shouldn't contain the same things.

The framing that unlocked this: **seniority changes the evidence you're
looking for, not the question itself.**

Same question — "tell me about a project you're proud of" — different
expectations underneath. That's better than the obvious alternative, which is
having separate junior and senior question banks. Real interviews don't do
that. A senior candidate gets asked the same opening question as everyone
else; what differs is what the interviewer is listening for.

## My first attempt, and why I rejected it

I tried to articulate the levels and got:

- **Junior** — a project where they learned a lot
- **Mid** — a project where they produced good quality work they're happy with
- **Senior** — a project where they led a process and solved cross-collaboration

Then I said it didn't feel quite right, and couldn't immediately say why. What
I eventually landed on was that it lacked nuance — not enough for an agent to
act on. "Led a process" could describe half the mid-level designers I've
taught.

The correction that mattered came next. Someone suggested senior meant
spotting the real problem, reframing it, bringing people along.

**A senior isn't a lead.**

That distinction is the single most important thing in this calibration, and
most interview frameworks get it wrong. They treat the career ladder as a
march toward managing people, so "senior" collapses into "leads a team." That
makes the product useless for exactly the people most likely to use it —
experienced individual contributors who have never managed anyone and have no
intention of starting.

Senior IC work is depth of reasoning and impact *without formal authority*:
framing, ambiguity, trade-offs, cross-functional influence, systems thinking,
judgment. Influence without headcount.

## The line I couldn't find

Junior was easy. I've taught enough junior designers to know the shape: I
learned a lot, I grew, I'm starting to be able to articulate what I did.

Senior got clear once "isn't a lead" was settled.

**Mid-level is where I was stuck**, and it's the transition that actually
matters — the one people are usually interviewing to make. What I eventually
wanted was: not just following a process, but *choosing* one, and being able
to explain why. Adjusting to constraints. Showing outcomes. Collaborating
deliberately rather than incidentally.

The compressed version that went into the calibration:

| Level | The claim the evidence should support |
|---|---|
| **Junior** | "I'm learning how to design." |
| **Mid-level** | "I'm making design decisions." |
| **Senior** | "I'm exercising design judgment in complexity." |

Three sentences took a long time and a rejected first draft.

## What the agent does with it

It doesn't hardcode canned follow-ups per level. The expectations sit in the
background and inform one decision: whether a gap is worth probing.

If a senior candidate never explains why the problem mattered, that's a
critical gap and worth a follow-up. The same omission from a junior candidate
might be fine — they're being assessed on clarity and learning, not problem
framing.

Which means calibration isn't scoring. It's the thing that decides where the
agent's attention goes.

## One caveat I insisted on

The calibration is a **configurable product rubric, not universal hiring
truth.** Seniority expectations vary by company, team, and market. A tool that
presents its own matrix as what all employers require would be confidently
miscalibrating people against a standard nobody agreed to.

It's my best synthesis from years of teaching. It's versioned, it's tunable,
and it should be argued with.
